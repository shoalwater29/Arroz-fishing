The classes that matter most in our waters: seagrass and macroalgae are the weed beds (whiting, squid, garfish country); reef and consolidated substrate holds the snapper; unconsolidated / sand is the flathead drift ground and the patches you cast to inside the weed.

Legend is served live by Seamap Australia, so the swatches always match the map exactly. It lists every class in the national scheme; only a handful appear in Port Phillip, Western Port and the local coast.

Catch probability is a relative bite index (0 to 100), not literal odds. Evidence grades vary: the temperature gates (VFA acoustic tracking), tide-flow rules and light windows are well supported; barometric pressure is weighted toward its trend because the science suggests fish respond to the weather change itself rather than the pressure number, and pressure is ignored for species without swim bladders (gummies, squid); moon is deliberately a whisper, not a driver, because long-run catch datasets disagree about it. Recommended windows also carry a small human-comfort tiebreak (up to 3 points) built on feels-like temperature, which includes wind chill, so between two near-equal windows the kinder one wins. The index: it blends barometric pressure level and 3-hour trend, water temperature against each species' preferred band (snapper fire at 16 to 19°C in the bay), tide movement, light, season, moon, and each spot's wind and swell exposure. Pressure is weighted heavily for snapper and hardly at all for gummy sharks, which have no swim bladder. Forecast data from Open-Meteo; satellite sea temperature (NASA JPL MUR, 1 km) and chlorophyll (NASA MODIS) via NOAA CoastWatch, used in place of model water temperature when available; the tide curve is modelled sea level (allow 20 to 30 minutes against BOM tables).
